Why This Pack Starts on Earth

Chris’s position, in his own terms: the effort should go to Earth first — to the oceans, the living things on this planet, and the pollution in front of us — rather than to Mars. That is his view. This pack does not argue against space exploration or against anyone who holds a different view; it simply puts its energy where Chris believes it belongs: here, on the one home we already have.

How to use this pack

  • Read pages 3–4 first. They give the scale of the problem, with every number sourced. If a number is not sourced, it is not in this pack.
  • Then pick one checklist — home (pages 6–8), shopping (page 9), or microplastics (page 5). Do that one before starting another.
  • Then pick one cleanup (pages 10–11). One morning on a beach or riverbank counts, and it is the part of this pack that removes plastic that is already out there.
  • Track it on page 13. Small, repeated actions beat one big effort that stops.

What this pack does not promise

  • It does not promise that one household can clean the ocean. No household can. The numbers on page 3 say why — and why the household steps still matter.
  • It does not promise health results from cutting microplastics. The World Health Organization’s 2019 review found microplastics in drinking water but judged the health risk low at the levels then measured, while calling the evidence limited and asking for more research. Cutting plastic is a sensible precaution, not a proven cure.
  • It does not tell you what to believe about anything else. It sticks to plastic, pollution, and cleanups.

The Facts — Checked

Every number below was checked against the source named beside it in November 2026. Figures are estimates and differ between studies; where two good sources disagree, the pack gives a range and says so.

How much plastic reaches the ocean

  • About 8 million metric tons of plastic enter the ocean every year — a widely used estimate from a University of Georgia study, reported by NOAA Ocean Today. Other recent estimates put the yearly inflow at 8–10 million tonnes (UNEP / UNCTAD, 2025).
  • An estimated 75–199 million tonnes of plastic are already in the ocean (UNEP, via UNCTAD, 2025). That is the stock already there, on top of each year’s new inflow.
  • Plastic makes up about 80% of all marine debris (UNEP, 2016, as cited in later reviews). Most of it comes from land — rivers, streets, and storm drains — not from ships.

What happens to plastic waste

  • Only 9% of plastic waste is recycled worldwide, after losses during recycling are counted (OECD, Global Plastics Outlook, 2022). In the same report: about 19% was incinerated, about 50% went to landfill, and about 22% was mismanaged.
  • Plastic waste more than doubled between 2000 and 2019, to 353 million tonnes, while plastics use nearly doubled to 460 million tonnes in 2019 (OECD, 2022). Recycling alone cannot keep up with that growth — using less, and reusing more, has to come first.
  • Plastic does not disappear. It breaks into smaller and smaller pieces. That is why a bottle thrown away decades ago can still be in the environment today as fragments.

The Great Pacific Garbage Patch

  • It is a real accumulation of floating debris in the North Pacific, about 1.6 million square kilometres in area, holding about 80,000 tonnes of plastic and about 1.8 trillion pieces (Lebreton et al., Scientific Reports, 2018).
  • About 46% of it by mass is fishing nets (same study). It is not a solid island you can walk on — most of it is small pieces spread through the water.
What was left out on purpose: this pack does not repeat the familiar “X animals die each year” figures, because the commonly quoted versions could not be traced to a single verified source in the time available. The problem is large enough on the numbers above without them.

What Is Found — and Where It Comes From

The five most common items in beach cleanups

From Ocean Conservancy’s International Coastal Cleanup data, as reported by NOAA: plastic cigarette butts, food wrappers, plastic beverage bottles, plastic bottle caps, and plastic straws & drink stirrers. That list is useful — it is also the shopping list for the swaps on pages 6–9. The most common litter is everyday, single-use stuff, not exotic waste.

How litter reaches the sea from inland places

  • Streets and parking lots — wind and rain wash litter into storm drains, which lead to creeks and rivers.
  • Rivers — a bottle dropped miles inland can travel downstream to the coast. Cleaning a riverbank is ocean cleanup.
  • Overflowing bins and loose trash day — light plastic blows out of open bins and truck beds before it ever reaches a landfill.
  • Laundry and drains — synthetic clothes shed fibres; see the microplastics page (page 5).
  • Fishing and boating gear — lost nets, lines, and traps keep fishing after they are lost. If you fish or boat, take damaged gear home and bin it.

Cleanups work — and their limits

  • International Coastal Cleanup: run by Ocean Conservancy since 1986, on the third Saturday of September each year. Over its first four decades, nearly 20 million volunteers removed more than 405 million pounds of trash from beaches and waterways (Ocean Conservancy, October 2026). Volunteers also log what they find, which is how the “most common items” list above exists.
  • Texas Adopt-A-Beach: run by the Texas General Land Office since 1986. More than 600,000 volunteers have picked up more than 10,000 tons of trash from Texas beaches. At the fall 2026 coastwide cleanup (19 September 2026), 6,106 volunteers removed nearly 62,000 pounds.
  • The limit: cleanups remove what is already there. They do not stop the next 8 million tonnes. That is why this pack pairs cleanups with the home steps — one removes, the other reduces.
Kingwood note: Kingwood is inland, on Lake Houston. Litter here reaches the ocean through local creeks and the San Jacinto River system. A cleanup on a Lake Houston shore or a local creek counts — it is upstream work.

Microplastics — Plainly

What they are

Microplastics are plastic pieces or fibres smaller than 5 millimetres — about the size of a pencil eraser or smaller (NOAA Ocean Service). There are two kinds:

  • Primary microplastics — made small on purpose: resin pellets for manufacturing, and microbeads once used in cosmetics. Microbeads in rinse-off cosmetics were banned in the United States by the Microbead-Free Waters Act of 2015 (NOAA).
  • Secondary microplastics — larger plastic that has broken down in sun, wind, and water into smaller and smaller fragments. Most microplastics in the ocean are this kind.

What is known about health

  • Microplastics have been found in tap water and in bottled water. Different studies count different sizes, so their particle counts are not directly comparable — a study that can see smaller particles reports far more of them.
  • The World Health Organization’s 2019 review, Microplastics in drinking-water, concluded that microplastics in drinking water did not appear to present a health concern at the levels then measured, but that the evidence was limited and more research was needed. Its 2022 review again stressed uncertainty about exposure and nanoplastics.
  • What this pack does not claim: it does not claim microplastics cause any named disease, and it does not claim any filter or bottle removes them all. Anyone selling that certainty is ahead of the evidence.

Microplastic reduction checklist

  • Wash synthetic clothes (polyester, nylon, fleece) less often, in full loads, on cold, short cycles
  • Use a laundry bag or filter made to catch fibres, if you buy one — check it fits your machine first
  • Air-dry fleece and synthetics when you can; tumble dryers shed fibres too
  • Choose natural fibres (cotton, wool, linen) when replacing worn-out clothes, if the price works
  • Use a reusable water bottle instead of single-use bottled water — bottled water has repeatedly measured as high as, or higher than, tap water for plastic particles
  • Do not use rinse-off scrubs or toothpaste with plastic microbeads — check the ingredients for “polyethylene”
  • Keep tyres properly inflated and drive smoothly — tyre wear is a source of tiny plastic/rubber particles (general guidance; no particle count is claimed here)
  • Sweep up, do not hose down, plastic dust and fragments in the garage or driveway

At Home — Cut the Big Five First

These are the items that show up most in cleanups. Cutting them is the highest-return change a household can make, because each one is used daily and thrown away daily.

1. Bottles & caps

  • Keep a reusable bottle by the door and one in the car / bag once
  • Refill at home before leaving, instead of buying a bottle out daily
  • Keep caps on bottles when recycling — check your local rules first, as they differ by city
  • Switch to a water filter jug or tap filter if your tap water tastes off, instead of buying bottled water

2. Bags

  • Keep reusable bags in the car and by the front door once
  • Say no to a bag for one or two items you can carry daily
  • Reuse the plastic bags you already have as bin liners or for pet waste, until they are gone
  • Use cloth produce bags, or skip the produce bag for fruit with skin (bananas, oranges, avocados)

3. Food wrappers & takeout

  • Choose the unwrapped option when the price is the same (loose fruit, bakery bread, bulk bins)
  • Ask for “no straw, no extra cutlery” when ordering takeout or delivery
  • Keep a reusable fork, spoon, and straw in your bag or car if you eat out often
  • Pack lunch in a reusable container at least 3 days a week

4. Straws & stirrers

  • Drink without a straw when you can; keep one reusable straw if you need one
  • Stir coffee with a spoon, not a plastic stirrer

5. Cigarette butts (if anyone in the house smokes)

  • Use a pocket ashtray or a tin with a lid — never drop butts on the ground or out of a car window
  • Bin butts in household trash, not in a drain or on a beach. Butts are plastic (cellulose acetate) and are the single most-collected item in coastal cleanups.

At Home — Kitchen & Bathroom

Kitchen

  • Use cloths and a mop instead of single-use wipes where you can
  • Store leftovers in reusable containers or jars, not cling film, where practical
  • Buy the larger size of things you use every week, if it is cheaper per unit and you will finish it
  • Keep a small bin for clean, dry recycling next to the trash — if it is not next to it, it will not get used
  • Check your city’s recycling list before you bin anything — putting the wrong plastic in recycling can spoil a whole load. When in doubt, leave it out.
  • Compost food scraps if your city or a local group offers it; food in landfill does not help, and it keeps bins cleaner

Bathroom

  • Switch one product at a time to a bar or refill: soap, shampoo, or conditioner
  • Use a refillable bottle for liquid soap instead of buying a new pump bottle each time
  • Choose a bamboo toothbrush when your plastic one wears out — do not throw a working brush away early
  • Use washable cloths or a washable pad instead of single-use cotton rounds, if that suits you
  • Never flush wipes, cotton buds, or dental floss — even wipes labelled “flushable” can block drains and reach waterways

Laundry room

  • Wash full loads, cold, on a short cycle when clothes are not heavily soiled
  • Use a laundry bag for synthetics (see page 5)
  • Air-dry when the weather allows — it also saves on the power bill
  • Buy detergent in the largest refill or cardboard-box size you can use up
Remember the 9%: only about 9% of plastic waste is ultimately recycled (OECD, 2022). “Recyclable” on a label is not the same as “recycled.” Reduce and reuse first; recycling is the third step, not the first.

What I changed, and whether it stuck

If a swap does not work

  • Drop it and try a different one. A swap you abandon in week 2 helps nobody.
  • Do not buy a drawer full of “eco” replacements for things that still work. Using what you have is the lowest-plastic choice.
  • Cost matters: if the plastic-free version costs much more, keep the cheaper one and cut plastic somewhere free instead — refusing a bag, a straw, or a wrapper costs nothing.

Shopping — Lower-Plastic, Without Paying More

Before you shop

  • Bags, bottle, and containers packed 2 min
  • List written — impulse buys are usually the most packaged buys
  • Check what is already at home, so you do not double-buy

In the store

  • Loose fruit and vegetables before bagged ones, when the price is close
  • Cardboard, glass, or metal packaging before plastic, when the price is close — and only if you will reuse or recycle it
  • One large pack before several small packs of the same thing
  • Refill station first, if your store has one — bring a clean, dry container and check the price per unit
  • Skip the extra bag at the till; carry small items or use a bag you brought

Labels — what they do and do not mean

LabelWhat it means
“Recyclable”It can be recycled somewhere. It does not mean your city takes it, or that it will be recycled. Check your city’s list.
“Made from recycled plastic”Some of the plastic was recycled before. Better than new plastic, but still plastic.
“Biodegradable” / “compostable”It may need industrial composting heat to break down, and may not break down in the sea or in a home compost heap. Do not litter it.
No label at allAssume it is not recycled in your area unless your city’s list says otherwise.
Do not be fooled by “green” packaging: a paper-looking wrapper can have a plastic lining, and a “plant-based” bottle is still a single-use bottle. The lowest-plastic choice is usually the one with the least packaging of any kind.

Find a Cleanup — Anywhere

You do not need to live on the coast. Inland cleanups on rivers, lakes, and creeks keep plastic out of the ocean before it gets there.

Step 1 — Pick a route

  • Join an organised cleanup. Easiest first time: someone else brings bags, gloves, and a plan.
  • Do a solo cleanup. One person, one bag, one stretch of shore or path. Log what you find if you can — counts help.
  • Start your own. Pick a place, invite a few people, set a time, and tell the local parks department if it is public land.

Step 2 — Find the group

  • Ocean Conservancy — International Coastal Cleanup. Held every year on the third Saturday of September. Use Ocean Conservancy’s cleanup map to find a site near you, or download its Clean Swell app to log what you collect on a solo cleanup.
  • Surfrider Foundation. Volunteer-led chapters run beach cleanups through the year on the West, East, Gulf, Great Lakes, Hawaiian, and Puerto Rican coasts. Find a chapter through Surfrider’s chapter locator and check its events calendar.
  • Your city or county parks department. Search for “[your city] river cleanup” or “[your county] park cleanup” — many run spring and fall events that are not listed nationally.
  • Keep America Beautiful affiliates / local litter groups. Many towns have a volunteer litter-pick group that meets monthly.

Step 3 — Before you go

  • Check the date, meeting point, and time on the organiser’s own page — do not rely on a forwarded post
  • Check whether you need to register, and whether children are welcome
  • Gloves, sturdy closed shoes, sun protection, water in a reusable bottle, a hat
  • A bucket or reusable bag for collecting, plus a separate bag for recycling if the organiser asks for it
  • Phone charged, in case you need directions or first aid
Check before you travel: dates, meeting points, and registration rules change. Confirm on the organiser’s own site or by contacting them the week of the event.

Texas Cleanups — and Staying Safe

Texas Adopt-A-Beach (Texas General Land Office)

The Texas programme began in 1986 and is the state coordinator for the International Coastal Cleanup in Texas. Coastwide cleanups are held in spring (April) and fall (September), with a February cleanup in South Texas. As published by the General Land Office in 2026, the next dates after this pack was written were: 5 February 2027 (South Padre Island), 6 February 2027 (Coastal Bend), 17 April 2027 (coastwide), and 18 September 2027 (coastwide). Check the Texas Adopt-A-Beach site before travelling — dates and sites can change.

Groups can also “adopt” a specific Texas beach and clean it three times a year for two years. If you live near the coast and want a regular commitment, that is the route to ask about.

If you are in Kingwood / Houston

  • Nearest coast is Galveston — about an hour’s drive. Adopt-A-Beach sites there are the closest organised beach cleanups.
  • Closer to home: Lake Houston shores, local creeks, and Houston bayous. Search for Houston-area river and bayou cleanups — they are the upstream end of the same problem.
  • Do not wait for a beach trip to start. A creek cleanup this weekend counts.

Safety — read before any cleanup

  • Wear gloves at all times; do not pick up anything with bare hands
  • Wear closed, sturdy shoes — never sandals on a cleanup
  • Do not pick up syringes, chemicals, or anything leaking — mark the spot and tell the organiser
  • Do not lift anything heavy alone; do not wade into deep or fast water
  • Stay out of dunes and nesting areas, and do not disturb wildlife
  • Drink water, take shade breaks, and stop if you feel unwell in the heat
  • Wash hands after finishing, before eating or touching your face
  • Children stay with an adult at all times; check the organiser’s age rules first

Log What You Find — It Matters

Counting what you collect is not paperwork for its own sake. Cleanup counts are how we know cigarette butts, wrappers, bottles, caps, and straws are the top five items — and that knowledge is what the checklists in this pack are built on.

Solo cleanup — 60 minutes

  • Pick a stretch: one beach section, one park path, or one creek bank 5 min
  • Walk it once, collecting everything that does not belong there 40 min
  • Sort into trash and recycling as the organiser (or your city’s rules) asks 5 min
  • Count the top items and write them in the table below 5 min
  • Bin the bags properly; wash your hands 5 min
