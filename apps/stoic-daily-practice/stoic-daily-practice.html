The dichotomy of control

Epictetus opens his Enchiridion with one distinction: some things are in our power, and others are not. Everything in this exercise hangs on it.

In my control: my judgments, my intentions, my desires and aversions, my words and actions — in short, what I choose.
Not in my control: my body’s health, my property, my reputation, other people’s opinions and choices, the past, the outcome — in short, what merely happens.

      When you are unsure

      Ask: “Is there an action I can take that directly changes this?” If yes, it belongs — at least in part — in your control, and the action is the thing to do. If no action of yours changes it, it belongs outside your control, and the work is your judgment about it: the story you tell yourself, and whether you will let it rule the day. Many worries split in two — sort each part separately.

        About Stoicism

        Stoicism is a practical philosophy for living well — not a set of cold sayings, but a daily discipline. It began in Athens around 300 BC, taught by Zeno of Citium in a painted porch (a stoa), which is where the school got its name.

        The core ideas

        Virtue is the only true good. The Stoics held that the only thing truly good is a good character — and the only thing truly bad is a bad one. Wealth, health, reputation, and comfort are not good or bad in themselves; they are materials to be used well or badly. The four cardinal virtues are wisdom (seeing clearly), justice (treating others fairly), courage (doing what is right even when it is hard), and temperance (self-control and wanting only what is enough).

        The dichotomy of control. Some things are in our power — our judgments, intentions, and actions. Most things are not — other people, the past, outcomes, fortune. Suffering, the Stoics argued, mostly comes not from events but from demanding that what is not in our power should obey us. Freedom is wanting only what is yours to choose.

        Amor fati — love of fate. Do not merely endure what happens; accept it and work with it. Epictetus put it plainly: do not demand that events happen as you wish, but wish them to happen as they do happen. This is not passivity — it is refusing to waste strength fighting what has already happened, so that strength is free for what you can still do.

        Memento mori — remember you will die. Marcus Aurelius reminded himself daily that death hangs over us and that life is short. The point is not gloom but urgency and gratitude: to stop postponing the good life, to treat each day as if it could be the last — calmly, not frantically — and to value the time that is actually ours.

        Examine your impressions. Between an event and your reaction sits a judgment — a story you add. Epictetus taught his students to pause at each harsh impression and say: “You are an appearance, and in no manner what you appear to be.” Then check it: is this in my control? Is my judgment true? That pause is the whole practice.

        The three voices in this app

        Marcus Aurelius (AD 121–180) — Roman emperor from AD 161 until his death. His Meditations are private notes to himself, written in Greek during military campaigns, never meant for publication. They are reminders, not lectures: a powerful man correcting himself.

        Epictetus (c. AD 50–135) — born enslaved in Rome, freed, and later a teacher in Nicopolis. He wrote nothing himself; his student Arrian recorded his teaching in the Discourses, and distilled it into the short handbook, the Enchiridion (“that which is at hand”).

        Seneca (c. 4 BC – AD 65) — Roman statesman, playwright, and adviser to the emperor Nero. Late in life he wrote 124 moral letters to his friend Lucilius, on time, fear, money, friendship, and death — practical advice from a man who knew wealth and power from the inside and found them wanting.
