How this works

  1. You pick a philosopher and a claim.
  2. The page shows your position, then reveals the philosopher’s answer in three moves: an opening quote, a counter-quote, and a closing quote.
  3. Every quoted line is copied verbatim from the edition named under it. Square brackets are the translator’s own. An ellipsis (…) means words were cut from the passage — at the start, or from the middle — never added, and never from the end: every quote runs to the end of its sentence.
  4. Nothing on this page is generated in the philosopher’s voice. The framing sentences are modern, plain, and labelled as framing.
  5. This file works offline. It makes no network calls, needs no account, and stores nothing.
What this page deliberately does NOT do
  • It does not use the popular modern line “You have power over your mind” — that wording has no verbatim source in the Meditations and is excluded. The nearest real passage, Meditations 8.47, is quoted instead, in George Long’s words.
  • It does not invent, paraphrase-into-quotes, or “modernise” a line and call it a quotation.
  • It does not use voice, a clone, or any likeness of a living person.
  • Where Seneca is quoting someone else (Cleanthes in Letter 107, Epicurus in Letter 16, Lucilius in Letter 24), the page says so.

Sources — the exact editions used

  • Marcus Aurelius, Meditations — translated by George Long, 1862. Project Gutenberg eBook 15877, The Thoughts of the Emperor Marcus Aurelius Antoninus. Cited by book and section, e.g. 4.17.
  • Epictetus, Enchiridion (The Manual) — translated by George Long, in A Selection from the Discourses of Epictetus, with the Encheiridion. Project Gutenberg eBook 10661. Cited by chapter, e.g. ch. 8. Arrian compiled the teaching; Epictetus himself wrote nothing.
  • Seneca, Letters to Lucilius (Moral Letters to Lucilius) — translated by Richard M. Gummere, Loeb Classical Library, 1917–1925. Cited by letter and section, e.g. Letter 1.2. The Gummere text is also carried on Wikisource under Moral letters to Lucilius.

Check any quote: open the edition above, go to the book / chapter / letter named, and compare the words. That is the whole verification method — and the reason the quote list on this page is short on purpose.
