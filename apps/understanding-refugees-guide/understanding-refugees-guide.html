How to Use This Guide

This guide is for anyone who wants to understand, in plain language, what it means to be forced from home — and what helps, and what does not. Read it in order, or dip into one section at a time. It works for personal reading, a classroom, a faith or community group, or a family conversation.

A note before you start

  • Refugees are people first. Behind every number in this guide are individuals and families — parents, children, grandparents, neighbours — whose lives were interrupted, not defined, by displacement.
  • This guide does not take a side in any conflict. It describes, factually, what forced displacement is and what people going through it commonly face.
  • Figures change. Displacement rises and falls as conflicts start and end and as people return home. The figures here are dated, so you can see when they were true. Check the UNHCR figures page for the latest numbers before quoting them to others.
  • Nothing in this guide is legal advice. Rules about asylum and resettlement differ by country and change often. Anyone facing those decisions should get advice from a qualified legal adviser in their own country.
Language matters. Words like refugee, asylum seeker, migrant and internally displaced person mean different things. Page 3 explains each one, using the definitions of UNHCR, the UN Refugee Agency.

Words That Matter

These words are often used as if they meant the same thing. They do not. The definitions below follow UNHCR, the UN Refugee Agency.

WordWhat it means
RefugeeA person forced to flee their own country because of persecution, war or violence, who has a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, political opinion or membership of a particular social group, and who cannot safely return home. Refugees have crossed an international border and are recognised under international law as needing protection.
Asylum seekerA person who has fled their country and asked another country for protection, whose claim for refugee status has not yet been decided. Every refugee was once an asylum seeker; not every asylum seeker is recognised as a refugee.
Internally displaced person (IDP)A person forced to leave home who has not crossed an international border. IDPs remain inside their own country. The difference between a refugee and an IDP is whether an international border was crossed.
MigrantA broad term, not defined in international law, for a person who moves away from their usual place of residence, whether inside a country or across a border, and whether the move was forced or voluntary. Moving to find work or improve living standards is not, by itself, what makes someone a refugee.
Stateless personA person who is not considered a national by any country under the operation of its law.

Why the words matter

  • A refugee has legal protection under international law. An asylum seeker is still waiting for that recognition. An IDP may have very limited protection, because they are still under their own government — which can be part of the problem.
  • Calling someone a migrant when they were forced to flee can hide the fact that returning home is not safe for them.
  • When you are unsure which word fits, say what you know: “a person who was forced to leave home.” That is accurate, and it keeps the person, not the label, in view.

Sources: UNHCR definitions, as summarised in UNHCR and ShelterBox explainers on refugee, migrant, asylum seeker and IDP. The refugee definition comes from the 1951 Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees.

The Numbers — With Sources

Displacement is counted by UNHCR, the UN Refugee Agency, in its annual Global Trends report. The figures below are from Global Trends 2024, released in June 2025, unless stated otherwise. Figures are rounded by UNHCR, so the parts do not always add up exactly to the total.

FigureNumberWhen
People forcibly displaced worldwide123.2 millionEnd of 2024 (UNHCR)
Refugees living beyond their borders42.7 millionEnd of 2024 (UNHCR)
People displaced inside their own country (IDPs, conflict and violence)73.5 millionEnd of 2024 (UNHCR / IDMC)
Asylum seekers (claims not yet decided)8.4 millionEnd of 2024 (UNHCR)
People forcibly displaced worldwide122.1 millionEnd of April 2025 (UNHCR)
People who returned home in 20249.8 million — 1.6 million refugees and 8.2 million IDPs2024 (UNHCR)

What the numbers mean

  • About 1 in every 67 people on Earth was forcibly displaced at the end of 2024 (UNHCR, Global Trends 2024).
  • Most displaced people stay close to home. 73 per cent are hosted in low- and middle-income countries, and 67 per cent are hosted in neighbouring countries (UNHCR, Global Trends 2024).
  • The largest displacement situations at the end of 2024 were Sudan (14.3 million refugees and IDPs), Syria (13.5 million), Afghanistan (10.3 million) and Ukraine (8.8 million) (UNHCR, Global Trends 2024).
  • Displacement has roughly doubled in a decade (UNHCR, June 2025). The main drivers named by UNHCR in June 2025 were large conflicts, including in Sudan, Myanmar and Ukraine, and the continued failure to stop the fighting.
  • Returns happen too. 9.8 million forcibly displaced people returned home in 2024 — the most refugees returning in more than two decades. UNHCR notes that many returns happened in difficult conditions, so return is not always the end of hardship.
Read numbers carefully. “Forcibly displaced people” (123.2 million) is the total. “Refugees” (42.7 million) is only one part of it. The two are not interchangeable.

Why People Flee

People do not leave home lightly. Most have lived in the same place for years, with a home, work, family and neighbours. Fleeing usually means leaving most of that behind, often at short notice, and not knowing when — or whether — return will be possible. UNHCR counts people as forcibly displaced when they flee persecution, conflict, violence, human rights violations, or events seriously disturbing public order.

The main reasons, in plain terms

  • War and armed conflict. Fighting makes a home unsafe, destroys housing, schools and hospitals, and cuts off food, water and power. Families leave to stay alive.
  • Persecution. Being targeted for who you are, what you believe, or what you say — for reasons of race, religion, nationality, political opinion or membership of a particular social group.
  • Violence and serious disorder. Widespread violence, or a breakdown of public order, can make everyday life impossible even away from a battlefield.
  • Human rights violations. When basic rights — safety, freedom, a fair trial, freedom of speech and belief — are taken away, staying can become dangerous.

What people often have to leave behind

  • Their home, and most of their belongings — they can usually carry only what they can hold or wear.
  • Their job and income, and often their savings, documents and qualifications.
  • Family members, friends and neighbours — sometimes without knowing where they are.
  • School for their children, medical care, and the routines of daily life.
  • A sense of safety and of knowing what tomorrow will bring.
A choice that is not a choice. A refugee’s journey is usually forced, not chosen. That is the difference between fleeing and moving: one is driven by danger, the other is decided freely.

Source for the drivers of displacement: UNHCR, Global Trends 2024 (June 2025) and the UNHCR press release of June 2025.

The Journey, and Arriving Somewhere New

This page describes, in general terms, what forced displacement often involves. Individual experiences differ widely. No graphic detail is included here, and none is needed to understand the scale of what is lost and what has to be rebuilt.

Leaving

  • Many people leave quickly, with little time to plan, pack or say goodbye.
  • Travel may be long, expensive and uncertain, on foot, by bus, by boat or by other means — and it may involve more than one move.
  • Families can be separated on the way. Children may travel with one parent, with relatives, or alone.
  • Documents — passports, birth certificates, school and work records — can be lost, left behind or destroyed. That makes every step after arrival harder.

Arriving

  • Arrival does not mean safety is settled. Asylum seekers wait — often for months or years — for a decision on their claim, without knowing whether they will be allowed to stay.
  • Many displaced people live in towns and cities in a neighbouring country, not in camps. Others live in camps or temporary settlements, sometimes for years.
  • Everyday life has to be rebuilt from the start: somewhere to live, food, clean water, medical care, school for children, and a way to earn a living — in a place where the language, laws and customs may be unfamiliar.
  • Uncertainty itself is hard to live with: not knowing whether you can stay, work, study, or ever go home.
“What profit is there in crossing the sea and in going from one city to another? If you would escape your troubles, you need not another place but another personality.”
Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 104.8, trans. Richard M. Gummere. Seneca’s point is about the inner work that travel alone cannot do — the person travels with themself. It is not a claim that moving is pointless: for a refugee, moving is often what keeps a person alive.

Life in a New Place — and Poverty

Starting again in a new country is hard even when people are welcomed. Language, paperwork, housing, work and school all have to be learned at once, while coping with loss and uncertainty.

What rebuilding often involves

  • Language. Learning a new language takes time. Until then, everyday tasks — shopping, seeing a doctor, helping a child with school — are much harder.
  • Work. Qualifications earned at home may not be recognised. Many refugees take work below their skill level, or cannot work legally while their claim is decided.
  • Children and school. Children need schooling, stability and friends — and may have missed months or years of education.
  • Health. Medical care may be interrupted, and the stress of displacement itself takes a toll on physical and mental health.
  • Belonging. Being new, visibly different, or unable to speak the local language can bring loneliness, misunderstanding and, sometimes, hostility.

Poverty, in numbers

Poverty and displacement often go together — poverty can be part of why a place is unsafe, and displacement almost always makes people poorer, at least at first.

  • 824 million people — about one in ten people worldwide — were living in extreme poverty in 2024, on less than $3.00 per person per day (2021 purchasing power parity). Source: World Bank, September 2026 Global Poverty Update, Poverty and Inequality Platform.
  • Extreme poverty is defined by the World Bank as living on less than $3.00 a day. The line was raised from $2.15 in June 2025, so figures using the older line are not directly comparable.
  • Having little money does not mean having little dignity, skill or will. Many refugees were teachers, builders, farmers, nurses, shopkeepers and students before they fled — and many rebuild working lives in their new home.
What lasts. What people carry with them is not only loss: it is also skills, languages, faith, family ties, and the determination to rebuild. Help that recognises that — rather than treating refugees only as people in need — tends to work better.

Common Misunderstandings

These are things people often hear about refugees that do not match the facts. Each correction below is tied to the sources on page 4.

Often saidWhat the facts show
“Most refugees come to rich countries.”Most do not. 73 per cent are hosted in low- and middle-income countries, and 67 per cent in neighbouring countries (UNHCR, Global Trends 2024).
“Refugees are the same as migrants.”No. A refugee was forced to flee and cannot safely return home. A migrant may have moved freely, for work or a better life. See page 3.
“They are all asylum seekers.”Asylum seekers are one group among the forcibly displaced: 8.4 million at the end of 2024, out of 123.2 million forcibly displaced people (UNHCR).
“No one ever goes home.”People do return. 9.8 million forcibly displaced people returned home in 2024, including 1.6 million refugees (UNHCR). Return is not always safe or lasting, and it is not the only outcome — some people settle where they are.
“Refugees are only a burden.”Displacement is costly, for refugees and for host communities. But refugees also work, pay taxes, start businesses, and contribute skills and culture — and most host countries are not rich countries.
“It is a single crisis, in one place.”No. Displacement comes from many conflicts and situations at once. The largest at the end of 2024 were Sudan, Syria, Afghanistan and Ukraine — four different situations, in different parts of the world (UNHCR).

How to check a claim

  • Ask: who counted this, and when? A number without a source or a date is not a fact — it is a rumour.
  • Check whether “refugees” and “forcibly displaced people” are being used as if they were the same number. They are not.
  • Be wary of a single story presented as if it were true of everyone. One person’s experience is real; it is not a statistic.

How Ordinary People Can Help

You do not need money, power or a special role to help. Most of what helps is ordinary, local and human. This page lists general actions only. It names no organisation and links to no donation page — check any group yourself before giving money or time.

1. Learn, and share what is true

  • Read from reliable sources — UNHCR, the World Bank, and reputable news organisations — and share figures with their source and date.
  • Use the right words (page 3). Correct a misunderstanding gently, with a fact, rather than with an argument.
  • Listen to refugees themselves, in their own words, when they choose to share them.

2. Be welcoming where you live

  • Say hello. Invite a new neighbour for a meal, a walk, or a community event. Small, ordinary kindness is often what people remember.
  • Help with practical things you already know how to do: reading a letter, finding a bus route, practising the local language, explaining how a school or clinic works.
  • Welcome children: play, school help, and friendship matter as much as anything else.

3. Give time, skills and things — carefully

  • Offer a skill: language practice, help with a CV, driving, cooking, repairs, childcare, or homework help.
  • If you give goods, ask first what is actually needed. Unasked-for donations can create more work for the people receiving them.
  • If you give money, give it through a reputable, registered organisation you have checked yourself, and keep the receipt.

4. At work, school and in your community

  • Support fair chances: work, training and education for people who are allowed to work and study.
  • If your school, workplace or faith group is discussing refugees, bring facts and a human story, not slogans.
  • Speak up, calmly, when someone is treated badly because they are new or different.

5. Look after yourself, too

  • Reading about war and displacement is heavy. Take breaks, talk to someone you trust, and do not try to carry it alone.
  • Helping is a long effort, not a single act. Steady, small help over time counts more than a single grand gesture.
What not to do: do not share graphic images of suffering; do not ask a refugee to retell the worst part of their story for your curiosity; do not make promises — about housing, work or papers — that you cannot keep.

Voices From Long Ago — and Sources

People have been forced from home for as long as there have been cities and wars. These three short passages, from writers who lived nearly two thousand years ago, are about the same questions this guide raises: what is home, what can be taken from us, and what cannot.

“…But my nature is rational and social; and my city and country, so far as I am Antoninus, is Rome, but so far as I am a man, it is the world. The things then which are useful to these cities are alone useful to me.”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.44, trans. George Long, Project Gutenberg eBook 15877.
“…I must go into exile. Does any man then hinder me from going with smiles and cheerfulness and contentment?”
Epictetus, Discourses 1.1, trans. George Long, Project Gutenberg eBook 10661 (in the selection headed “Of the things which are in our power and not in our power”).
“…What profit is there in crossing the sea and in going from one city to another? If you would escape your troubles, you need not another place but another personality.”
Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 104.8, trans. Richard M. Gummere, Wikisource Moral letters to Lucilius.
